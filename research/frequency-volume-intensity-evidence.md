# Resistance-Training Frequency, Weekly Volume, and Intensity: Evidence Summary for Strength and Hypertrophy

Scope note: Research conducted 2026-10-21. Direct fetches of journal/PubMed pages were blocked by the session's egress proxy, so every figure below was obtained through search-engine retrieval of abstracts, open repositories, and citing secondary summaries (Stronger By Science, Biolayne REPS, MASS/Iron Culture, Examine). Where a number came only from a secondary summary and could not be checked against the abstract, it is flagged "(secondary, unverified)". Nothing below is fabricated; items I could not source are listed under Gaps.

Population caveat that applies throughout: most meta-analyses pool mostly untrained or recreationally trained subjects; the trained-lifter subset is thin. The reader (intermediate lifter) should weight trained-subject studies (Schoenfeld 2019 1/3/5-set trial, Brigatto 2022, Aube 2022, Hamarsland 2022, Colquhoun 2018, Enes 2024, Räntilä 2026) more heavily than pooled effect sizes.

## Key Question 1: Does training frequency per muscle matter when weekly volume is equated? Does 2x beat 1x? Does 3x+ add anything?

### Takeaway
When weekly set volume is held constant, frequency has little or no independent effect on hypertrophy (pooled slope ~0.3% per extra day, credible interval spanning zero), but a real, diminishing-returns effect on strength (Pelland et al. 2025/26, 67 studies). The 2016 "2x beats 1x" finding was driven by non-volume-equated studies; the 2019 update and the 2018 strength meta-analyses found the frequency effect disappears once volume is matched.

### Cited Findings

**Hypertrophy meta-analyses**
- Schoenfeld, Ogborn & Krieger 2016 (Sports Medicine; 10 studies; flagged as older foundational work): higher frequency produced a larger hypertrophy effect size than lower frequency (ES 0.49 ± 0.08 vs 0.30 ± 0.07, P = 0.002) on a binary high/low comparison; the authors concluded training a muscle ≥2x/week beats 1x but said whether 3x beats 2x "remains to be determined." They also could not generate reliable estimates for frequency-per-muscle subgroups because of sample size. — [PubMed 27102172](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27102172/); [Schoenfeld/lookgreatnaked summary](https://lookgreatnaked.com/blog/how-many-times-should-you-train-a-muscle-each-week)
- Schoenfeld, Grgic & Krieger 2019 (J Sports Sci 37(11):1286-1295; 25 studies, 836 participants, 6-30 wk): "resistance training frequency does not significantly or meaningfully impact muscle hypertrophy when volume is equated"; a significant effect favouring higher frequency appeared only when volume was NOT equated, and even then the 1 vs 3+ days/week difference was "modest." — [PMC table summarizing frequency reviews](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9302196/table/T1); [Biolayne REPS summary](https://biolayne.com/reps/issue-31/the-king-of-volume-metas/); [Stronger By Science frequency article](https://www.strongerbyscience.com/frequency-muscle/)
- Pelland, Remmert, Robinson, Hinson, Zourdos et al. (SportRxiv preprint 2024; published Sports Medicine, DOI 10.1007/s40279-025-02344-w, cited variously as 2025 and Feb 2026): 67 studies, 2,058 participants (hypertrophy: 35 studies, 220 effects, 1,032 participants; strength: 66 studies, 490 effects, 2,020 participants). Bayesian multi-level meta-regressions adjusted for duration and training status. Frequency effect on hypertrophy with volume in the model: beta = 0.32% per additional training day (95% CrI -0.14% to 0.82%), described as negligible; posterior probability of a positive frequency effect on hypertrophy was <100%, "compatible with negligible effects." — [SportRxiv preprint 460](https://sportrxiv.org/index.php/server/preprint/view/460); [train.fitness summary with coefficients](https://train.fitness/personal-trainer-blogs/not-all-sets-are-created-equal); [sci-sport summary](https://sci-sport.com/en/?p=95624)
- Hamarsland et al. 2022 (Frontiers in Physiology; trained participants): 21 completers (11 M, 10 F, age 25.9 ± 4.0), 9-week whole-body heavy RT, equal weekly volume at 2 sessions/week (n = 13) vs 4 sessions/week (n = 8). "No differences between groups were observed for any of the measures of muscle growth or muscle strength" (DXA lean mass, vastus lateralis ultrasound, 1RM squat/hack squat/bench/chest press). Authors: frequency is "less decisive when weekly training volume is equated." The paper's introduction summarizes the volume-equated trained literature: no difference for 3 vs 6/wk (Colquhoun 2018; Saric 2019), 2 vs 4/wk (Johnsen & van den Tillaar 2021), 1 vs 5/wk (Gomes 2019), with Zaroni 2019 (higher-frequency full-body beat split in trained men) the lone exception. — [Frontiers full text](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/physiology/articles/10.3389/fphys.2021.789403/full); [PMC8766679](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8766679)
- Colquhoun et al. 2018 (JSCR; trained men, 6 weeks): 3x/week vs 6x/week with identical weekly work; similar maximal strength and body-composition improvements. — [ResearchGate PDF](https://researchgate.net/profile/Jozo-Grgic/publication/327435932_Resistance_Training_Frequencies_of_3_and_6_Times_Per_Week_Produce_Similar_Muscular_Adaptations_in_Resistance-Trained_Men/links/5bd26d6892851c6b278f311d/Resistance-Training-Frequencies-of-3-and-6-Times-Per-Week-Produce-Similar-Muscular-Adaptations-in-Resistance-Trained-Men.pdf); [IDEA Fit summary](https://www.ideafit.com/volume-is-key-factor-in-building-strength)
- Johnsen & van den Tillaar 2021 (PeerJ; trained men, volume-matched 2 vs 4 sessions/week, 21 participants): no frequency advantage for strength. — [PeerJ 10781](https://peerj.com/articles/10781)

**Strength meta-analyses**
- Grgic, Schoenfeld, Davies, Lazinica, Krieger & Pedisic 2018 (Sports Medicine 48(5):1207-1220): across all studies, a dose-response between frequency and strength; when restricted to volume-equated studies, no significant effect (p = 0.421). Authors interpret higher frequency mainly as a vehicle for more weekly volume. — [Victoria Univ. repository](https://vuir.vu.edu.au/37695/); described in [PeerJ 10781](https://peerj.com/articles/10781) and [PMC7897409](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7897409)
- Ralston, Kilgore, Wyatt, Buchan & Baker 2018 (Sports Medicine-Open 4:36; 12 studies, 74 treatment groups): higher vs lower frequency, combined exercises: mean ES 0.09 (95% CI -0.06 to 0.24; p = 0.25), not significant. Volume-equated low vs high frequency: ES 0.03 (95% CI -0.20 to 0.27; p = 0.78). — [DOAJ record](https://doaj.org/article/5a0ee38a0bf847ee9329f9fe72d50f88); [Springer Open PDF](https://sportsmedicine-open.springeropen.com/track/pdf/10.1186/s40798-018-0149-9)
- Pelland et al. (above): frequency effect on strength had a 100% posterior probability of being positive even with volume in the model, with diminishing returns (going from 1 to 2 sessions/week helps more than 3 to 4). — [train.fitness summary](https://train.fitness/personal-trainer-blogs/not-all-sets-are-created-equal); [sci-sport summary](https://sci-sport.com/en/?p=95624). One secondary summary gives the slope as +3.27% strength gain per additional training day at average frequency (95% CrI 2.74%, 3.84%) (secondary, unverified; a second search could not locate this number). — [search summary citing Pelland follow-up](https://sportrxiv.org/index.php/server/preprint/view/460)
- Stronger By Science (Nuckols) reading of the literature: with volume equated, higher frequency "probably beneficial for strength, but probably didn't matter much for hypertrophy (past a frequency of twice or maybe thrice per week)"; if doing fewer than ~10 sets per muscle per week, once-per-week frequency "is probably perfectly fine"; the main value of higher frequency is enabling more volume and higher per-set quality. — [SBS frequency](https://www.strongerbyscience.com/frequency/); [SBS frequency-muscle](https://www.strongerbyscience.com/frequency-muscle/)

### Inferences
- For hypertrophy, the program designer should treat frequency as a volume-distribution tool, not an independent growth stimulus: 2x vs 3x vs 6x per muscle produces indistinguishable growth if weekly hard sets are the same (Hamarsland, Colquhoun, Pelland).
- The reason 1x/week looks worse in older work is that cramming all weekly volume into one session either (a) was confounded with lower volume, or (b) pushes per-session sets past the point of diminishing returns (see Question 2). So "1x is fine" holds at low weekly volume (<~10 sets), and "2x+" matters as weekly volume rises.
- For strength, more frequent exposure to the lift (skill practice, higher per-set quality under less fatigue) carries a modest independent benefit; a 3-day week should still touch each main lift 2x if strength is the priority.

### Gaps
- Exact Pelland strength-per-day slope (3.27%) could not be verified against the preprint/published abstract.
- Exact between-group effect sizes for Colquhoun 2018 and Hamarsland 2022 hypertrophy outcomes were not retrievable (only "no difference").
- No meta-analysis isolates 2x vs 3x vs 4x+ per muscle in trained lifters with volume equated; evidence rests on a handful of small trials (n = 8-21 per arm).

## Key Question 2: Weekly set-volume ranges per muscle for hypertrophy and for strength; dose-response, diminishing returns, per-session set ceiling

### Takeaway
Hypertrophy shows a positive but flattening dose-response: ~10+ weekly sets/muscle beats <5 (Schoenfeld 2017), 12-20 sets/muscle/week is the best-supported range for trained men (Baz-Valle 2022), and Pelland's meta-regression finds ~0.24% extra growth per added "fractional" weekly set with returns diminishing but not clearly plateauing within studied ranges. Strength saturates much sooner (per Pelland: pronounced diminishing returns; per Remmert: no detectable per-session benefit beyond ~2 direct sets). Per-session, hypertrophy gains become undetectable beyond ~11 fractional sets per muscle per session (Remmert 2025 preprint), supporting the ~8-10 hard sets/muscle/session guideline.

### Cited Findings

**Hypertrophy, weekly volume**
- Schoenfeld, Ogborn & Krieger 2017 (J Sports Sci 35(11); 15 studies, only 2 with trained subjects; online 2016): weekly sets per muscle <5, 5-9, and 10+ produced ~5.4%, 6.6%, and 9.8% muscle growth respectively (trend, p = 0.074); authors concluded "at least 10 weekly sets per muscle group" to maximize growth, with too little data above 10 to conclude further. — [paper PDF](https://paulogentil.com/pdf/Dose-response%20relationship%20between%20weekly%20resistance%20training%20volume%20and%20increases%20in%20muscle%20mass%20-%20A%20systematic%20review%20and%20metaanalysis.pdf); [lookgreatnaked summary](https://lookgreatnaked.com/blog/how-many-sets-do-you-need-to-perform-to-maximize-muscle-gains); [PMC6303131 commentary on limitations](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6303131/)
- Baz-Valle et al. 2022 (J Human Kinetics 81:199-210; 7 studies qualitative, 6 quantitative; young trained men): low (<12), moderate (12-20), high (>20) weekly sets. No difference moderate vs high for quadriceps (p = 0.19) or biceps (p = 0.59); high superior for triceps (p = 0.01). Conclusion: "12-20 weekly sets per muscle group may be an optimum standard recommendation" for young trained men. — [PMC8884877](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8884877/)
- Pelland et al. 2025/26 (above): fractional set counting (indirect sets = 0.5) fit hypertrophy best; direct counting fit strength best. Marginal slope: +0.24% muscle size per additional fractional weekly set at the mean volume of 12.25 sets (95% CrI 0.15%, 0.33%); 100% posterior probability of a positive volume slope for both outcomes; "both best fit models suggest diminishing returns, with the diminishing returns for strength being considerably more pronounced." One summary: ~4 fractional weekly sets suffices to exceed the smallest detectable hypertrophy effect (2.05%), with no clear plateau identified. Another summary reports a point of undetectable outcome superiority (PUOS) of ~31 fractional sets/week for hypertrophy and ~3 fractional sets/week for strength (secondary, unverified). — [SportRxiv 460](https://sportrxiv.org/index.php/server/preprint/view/460); [train.fitness](https://train.fitness/personal-trainer-blogs/not-all-sets-are-created-equal); [sci-sport](https://sci-sport.com/en/?p=95624)
- Schoenfeld, Contreras, Krieger et al. 2019 (MSSE; 34 trained men, 8 wk, 3 days/wk, 1 vs 3 vs 5 sets per exercise, i.e. ~6-9 / 18-27 / 30-45 weekly sets per muscle): all groups grew; greatest muscle thickness gains in the 5-set group; strength gains did not differ between groups ("volume enhances hypertrophy but not strength"). — [Lehman College release](https://www.lehman.edu/news/Lehman-Professors-Weight-Training-Study-Finds-Less-Will-Do.php); [UNM summary](https://www.unm.edu/~lkravitz/Article%20folder/VolumeHypertrophy.html)
- Brigatto et al. 2022 (trained men, 8 wk, 16 vs 24 vs 32 weekly sets): 32 sets produced greater lower-body and triceps thickness and lower-body strength than 16 sets; 1RM squat gains ~17% / 18% / 25%; 32 vs 24 not different (secondary French summary). — [sci-sport](https://sci-sport.com/?p=5120)
- Aube et al. 2022 (JSCR; 35 trained, 12 vs 18 vs 24 weekly lower-body sets, 2x/wk, 8 wk): thigh thickness did not grow more at 24 than 12 sets; 1RM squat gains reported as ~11% / 16% / 5% with 18 sets best (secondary; accounts conflict on hypertrophy ranking). — [PubMed 32058362](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32058362/); [Cissik summary](https://cissik.com/blog/?p=6940)
- Enes, De Souza & Souza-Junior 2024 (MSSE 56(3):553-563; trained males, 12 wk): starting at 22 weekly quad sets and adding 4 or 6 sets every 2 weeks (up to ~52 sets/wk) produced greater lower-limb strength than constant volume; hypertrophy results "interpret with caution." — [BarBend](https://barbend.com/news/you-dont-need-more-training-volume/); [NSCA PTQ](https://www.nsca.com/education/articles/ptq/training-volume-and-hypertrophy-an-evidence-based-approach-for-personal-trainers/)
- Räntilä & Ahtiainen 2026 (J Sci Sport Exerc; 27 strength-trained men; 10-wk run-in then 7-wk phase with 1.5x sets, ~60 sets/wk): no group difference in muscle size; only deadlift differed, favouring the LOWER-volume group; "substantially increasing RT volume did not enhance muscle hypertrophy or strength in athletes." — [JYX repository](https://jyx.jyu.fi/handle/123456789/112971)
- Barbalho et al. 2019 (trained women; 5, 10, 15, 20 sets per muscle per session): titled "Evidence for an upper threshold for resistance training volume in trained women" (note: this author group's data have since been questioned; cite cautiously). — [PubMed 30779716](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30779716/)

**Strength, weekly volume**
- Ralston, Kilgore, Wyatt & Baker 2017 (Sports Medicine; 9 studies): high vs low weekly sets, combined exercises ES 0.18 (95% CI 0.06-0.30; p = 0.003); isolation ES 0.23 (0.06-0.40; p = 0.008); group mean ES low = 0.83, medium = 0.98. Insufficient data to say whether 10-12 sets/week beats 5-9 (Examine). — [PMC5684266](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC5684266/); [Examine FAQ](https://examine.com/faq/how-much-volume-do-i-need-to-perform-to-increase-muscle-strength)
- Pelland (above): strength dose-response flattens far sooner than hypertrophy; direct sets are the best predictor.
- Androulakis-Korakakis et al. 2020 (meta-analysis, 6 studies, resistance-trained men): a single set of 6-12 reps at 70-85% 1RM, 2-3x/week to failure, over 8-12 weeks, produced "suboptimal, yet significant" 1RM squat and bench gains. Follow-up 2021 (powerlifters): ~3-6 working sets of 1-5 reps per week at >80% 1RM maintained/improved 1RM over 6-12 weeks. — [Substack summary of 2020](https://shortcutu.substack.com/p/setting-the-bar-low-but-still-making); [Frontiers 2021](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/sports-and-active-living/articles/10.3389/fspor.2021.713655/epub)

**Per-session ceiling**
- Remmert, Pelland, Robinson, Hinson & Zourdos 2025 (SportRxiv preprint 537, not peer reviewed; 67 studies; FAU): per-session volume showed 100% posterior probability of a positive slope for both outcomes, but PUOS (per-session sets beyond which more sets no longer have >50% likelihood of exceeding the smallest detectable effect) was ~11 fractional sets/session for hypertrophy and ~2 direct sets/session for strength (figures from Biolayne summary; FAU press release confirms "train smarter, not longer" framing). Layne Norton on the same data: "They didn't really find a cap" (i.e., slope stays positive, just undetectably small). — [SportRxiv 537](https://sportrxiv.org/index.php/server/preprint/view/537); [Biolayne REPS 36](https://biolayne.com/reps/issue-36/too-much-muscle-math-finding-the-ideal-training-volume-per-session/); [FAU news](https://fau.edu/newsdesk/articles/build-muscle-gain-strength-study.php); [BarBend](https://barbend.com/news/scientifically-correct-number-sets-per-workout/)
- Older Krieger meta-analyses (flagged older, 2009-2010) found set dose-response plateauing around 4-6 sets per exercise per session. — [PMC5969184 review](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5969184)
- No primary source was found for a formal "10 sets per muscle per session" cap; it appears only in secondary blogs. — [Alphaprogression blog (secondary)](https://alphaprogression.com/en/blog/sets-per-workout-maximum-muscle-gain)

### Inferences
- Practical hypertrophy range for an intermediate: 10-20 direct-equivalent hard sets per muscle per week, with ~12-16 as a default and 20+ reserved for lagging muscles or short overreaching blocks. The trained-lifter trials that pushed 30-50+ sets/week (Brigatto, Enes, Räntilä) show either small extra strength returns or nothing, with Räntilä showing a deadlift penalty.
- Per-session, cap each muscle at ~8-11 fractional hard sets (Remmert PUOS ~11; RP's practical 6-10); sets above that are near-zero marginal return and cost recovery. This is the mechanism by which frequency becomes relevant: 16 weekly sets fits in 2 sessions of 8, not 1 session of 16.
- Strength is cheap in volume: 2-6 heavy direct sets per lift per session, 2-3x/week, captures most of the strength signal (Remmert ~2 direct sets/session PUOS; Androulakis-Korakakis single-set data; Schoenfeld 2019 showing 1 set = 5 sets for strength).
- "Fractional" counting matters: for the designer, compound pressing counts ~0.5 set for triceps/front delts, rows/pulldowns ~0.5 for biceps/rear delts, when tallying weekly volume.

### Gaps
- Schoenfeld 2017's widely quoted "+0.37% hypertrophy per additional weekly set" could not be confirmed from retrieved text.
- Pelland's weekly PUOS values (~31 fractional sets hypertrophy; ~3 strength) are from one secondary summary and conflict with another summary's "no clear plateau"; the published Sports Medicine text was not accessible.
- Remmert per-session PUOS values (11 / 2) are from Biolayne's summary, not the preprint text.
- No meta-analysis separates upper- vs lower-body or large vs small muscle volume thresholds except Baz-Valle's triceps finding.

## Key Question 3: Renaissance Periodization volume landmarks (MV, MEV, MAV, MRV) and how well they match the research

### Takeaway
RP defines MV (maintenance), MEV (minimum effective), MAV (maximum adaptive), and MRV (maximum recoverable) as per-muscle weekly set counts and publishes only chest as a worked example on its own site (MV ~4-6, MEV ~6, MRV ~20 at 2 sessions / ~25 at 3); per-muscle tables circulating online are third-party reconstructions (e.g., back MEV 10 / MRV 25+, biceps 8 / 26+, quads 8 / 20+). The MEV-to-MRV band (roughly 6-25 sets) brackets the research's 10-20 range, but the per-muscle precision and the claim that volume should ramp weekly toward MRV are coaching heuristics rather than meta-analytic findings.

### Cited Findings
- RP definitions: MEV is "the lowest amount of training volume that produces measurable hypertrophy"; MRV is "the maximum volume you can perform and still recover from week to week"; MAV is the range between MEV and MRV where gains are best. RP general guidance: start at the low end and track recovery week to week; MEV around 6-8 direct sets/week for most muscles, MRV typically 20-25+. — [RP volume landmarks article](https://rpstrength.com/training-volume-landmarks-muscle-growth/); [arvo.guru explainer](https://arvo.guru/resources/volume-landmarks)
- RP chest (official): "perhaps around 4 sets per week may maintain chest size for experienced trainers" (help center) vs ~6 sets maintenance (landmarks article); MEV "at least 6 sets of direct chest work per week" for intermediate-advanced; MRV "around 20 sets per week with two sessions, rising to closer to 25 sets per week with three"; 1-3 chest exercises per session. — [RP help: Chest](https://help.rpstrength.com/hc/en-us/articles/32433153518359-Chest); [RP chest guide](https://rpstrength.com/chest-training-tips-hypertrophy/)
- Third-party per-muscle tables attributed to RP: back (thickness) MEV 6 / MAV 10-16 / MRV 20+; triceps 4 / 8-14 / 18+; hamstrings 4 / 8-14 / 16+; front delts 0 / 0-6 / 12 (front delts "rarely need direct work"). An unofficial Scribd compilation of Israetel's series: back MEV ~10, MRV 25+; biceps MEV ~8, MRV 26+; quads MEV ~8, MRV 20+. These differ from one another. — [arvo.guru calculator](https://arvo.guru/fr/tools/volume-calculator); [Scribd compilation](https://es.scribd.com/document/479545228)
- RP-style mesocycle: volume ramps from ~MEV in week 1 toward MRV over 4-6 weeks, then deload; the week-by-week tables found online are explicitly "illustrative." — [Coachway summary](https://coachway.io/articles/dr-mike-israetel-how-many-sets-for-coaches/)
- Research alignment: Baz-Valle's 12-20 sets/week and Schoenfeld's 10+ sets/week sit inside RP's MEV-MRV band; Pelland's "~4 fractional sets exceed the smallest detectable effect" is below most RP MEVs (6-10), and Räntilä 2026 / Aube 2022 show no benefit (or harm) near or above typical RP MRVs. — [PMC8884877](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8884877/); [JYX](https://jyx.jyu.fi/handle/123456789/112971)
- Biolayne (Norton) counterpoint: staying at 6-8 quality sets/week per muscle for long periods may be more productive than chasing the top of the range (coach opinion). — [BodySpec guide quoting Biolayne](https://www.bodyspec.com/blog/post/how_many_sets_per_muscle_group_weekly_volume_guide)

### Inferences
- The landmark framework is a useful scaffold (start ~MEV, progress, deload) but the numbers should be treated as starting points with ±5-set uncertainty; the literature does not support muscle-by-muscle MRVs to single-set precision.
- The RP chest example (MRV 20 at 2 sessions, 25 at 3) implicitly encodes the per-session ceiling of ~8-10 sets, consistent with Remmert's ~11 fractional-set PUOS.
- Evidence weakly supports progressive volume within a block for strength (Enes 2024) and is equivocal for hypertrophy; the designer can use RP ramps but should not expect research-backed superiority over flat volume at 12-16 sets.

### Gaps
- RP's own complete per-muscle landmark table could not be retrieved (behind their hypertrophy-guide series); all per-muscle numbers except chest are third-party.
- No peer-reviewed study has tested the MEV/MRV framework directly (e.g., individualized MRV-based vs fixed volume).

## Key Question 4: Rep ranges / loads (%1RM, RPE, RIR) for strength vs hypertrophy; training to failure vs reps in reserve

### Takeaway
Hypertrophy is load-independent across ~30-85% 1RM when sets are hard (Schoenfeld 2017: 21 studies; Lopez 2021: 28 studies, 747 adults), while 1RM strength is specific to heavy loads (Lopez: high and moderate > low; high vs moderate SMD 0.26-0.28, p = 0.068). Proximity to failure matters modestly for hypertrophy (closer = more growth, Robinson 2024) and not for strength (RIR slopes include zero); failure vs non-failure meta-analyses show no overall difference (Grgic 2022: strength ES -0.09, hypertrophy ES 0.22, both NS), with a small strength edge for non-failure when volume is uncontrolled (ES 0.34).

### Cited Findings

**Load**
- Schoenfeld, Grgic, Ogborn & Krieger 2017 (JSCR; 21 studies; low ≤60% 1RM vs high >60% 1RM, all to failure, ≥6 wk): 1RM strength significantly greater with high load; isometric strength no difference; hypertrophy similar between conditions. — [PubMed 28834797](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28834797/); [CKA summary](https://www.cka.ca/en/nlka-current-issues/strength-and-hypertrophy-adaptations-between-low--vs-high-load-resistance-training)
- Lopez et al. 2021 (MSSE 53(6):1206-1216; network meta-analysis, 28 studies, 747 adults; low >15RM, moderate 9-15RM, high ≤8RM, to failure): hypertrophy no difference between loads (overall P = .113-.469; subgroup P = .871-.995); strength superior for high and moderate vs low; high vs moderate non-significant (SMD 0.26-0.28, P = .068). Conclusion: hypertrophy largely load-independent; strength favours high load. — [JYX repository](https://jyx.jyu.fi/handle/123456789/75929)
- Schoenfeld, Grgic, Van Every & Plotkin 2021 (Sports 9(2):32): re-examination of the "repetition continuum" (heavy = strength, moderate = hypertrophy, light = endurance); concludes "muscular adaptations can be obtained, and in some cases optimized, across a wide spectrum of loading zones." — [Victoria Univ. open access](https://vuir.vu.edu.au/42418/); [DOAJ](https://doaj.org/article/6d51b905504d46f0873bb43d5d84bf21)

**Proximity to failure**
- Robinson, Pelland, Remmert, Refalo, Jukic, Steele & Zourdos 2024 (Sports Medicine 54(9):2209-2231; meta-regressions with estimated RIR; ~55 hypertrophy and 67 strength studies per secondary count): strength: in all best-fit models "the confidence intervals of the marginal slopes for estimated RIR contained a null point estimate," i.e., negligible relationship; hypertrophy: "seems to improve upon terminating sets closer to failure," modest, possibly non-linear. RIR values were estimated from study descriptions ("accuracy of this process is unknown"). — [Abertay repository](https://rke.abertay.ac.uk/en/publications/exploring-the-dose-response-relationship-between-estimated-resist/); [Biolayne REPS 32](https://biolayne.com/reps/issue-32/the-failure-factor-the-relationship-between-proximity-to-failure-and-strength-and-hypertrophy/)
- Grgic et al. 2022 (J Sport Health Sci; 15 studies, young adults): failure vs non-failure, strength ES -0.09 (95% CI -0.22 to 0.05), hypertrophy ES 0.22 (95% CI -0.11 to 0.55), both non-significant; non-failure groups often did more total volume when unmatched; in the 2-study trained subgroup, failure favoured hypertrophy (tentative). — [DOAJ record](https://doaj.org/article/dc36ba7ccd994798964f66b93bd8f662); [sci-sport summary](https://sci-sport.com/en/training-to-failure-or-not-impact-on-hypertrophy-and-strength/)
- Refalo et al. 2023 (proximity-to-failure systematic review/meta-analysis): no meaningful hypertrophy difference between failure and non-failure conditions (Biolayne summary); one secondary summary gives a small edge for closer-to-failure (ES 0.15-0.21) (secondary, unverified). — [Biolayne REPS 23](https://biolayne.com/reps/issue-23/is-training-to-failure-overrated/); [getfitcraft summary](https://getfitcraft.com/science/training-to-failure-vs-reps-in-reserve)
- Strength meta-analysis on repetition failure (likely Carvalho et al. 2022; authorship not confirmed in retrieved record): small pooled effect favouring NON-failure for strength (ES 0.34, p = 0.02); no significant effect in volume-controlled studies, trend still favouring non-failure. — [sport-iat record](https://lida.sport-iat.de/ta/Record/4039453)

**RPE / RIR tooling**
- Zourdos, Helms et al. 2016 (JSCR 30:267-275): RIR-based RPE scale (RPE 10 = 0 RIR, 9 = 1 RIR, etc.); strong inverse relationship between bar velocity and RPE in experienced squatters; "practical method to regulate daily training load." Lifters estimate RIR within ~1 rep when sets are 0-3 reps from failure; accuracy degrades further from failure. Traditional Borg RPE yields only 6.8-9.0 even at failure, motivating the RIR version. — [AUT repository (Zourdos 2016)](https://openrepository.aut.ac.nz/items/efef3b25-6701-4fb5-bb82-55fcd2a26027/full); [Helms 2018 Frontiers](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/physiology/articles/10.3389/fphys.2018.00247/pdf); [Helms load-selection accuracy](https://openrepository.aut.ac.nz/items/9d066ef2-8ebc-490b-af31-31b0fa9aca61/full)

### Inferences
- Strength work: 1-6 reps at ≥80% 1RM (RPE 7-9, 1-3 RIR), rarely to failure; failure adds fatigue without strength benefit (Robinson, Carvalho).
- Hypertrophy work: any load from ~30-85% 1RM (practically 6-20 reps, occasionally 20-30 for isolation) taken to 0-3 RIR; the last 1-2 sets of an exercise can go to failure, compounds generally at 1-3 RIR to protect volume and recovery.
- Because RIR estimates are only reliable within ~0-3 reps of failure, "RPE 7" compound sets are noisy; anchoring most hypertrophy sets at RPE 8-9 (1-2 RIR) is both evidence-aligned and measurable.

### Gaps
- Exact Robinson 2024 slope coefficients per RIR unit were not retrievable.
- Refalo 2023 effect sizes and the Carvalho 2022 authorship could not be confirmed from primary text.
- No high-quality trial compares RPE-anchored vs %1RM-anchored programming head-to-head for hypertrophy in intermediates.

## Key Question 5: Rest intervals between sets

### Takeaway
Rest >60 s gives a small hypertrophy benefit over shorter rest, with no further detectable benefit beyond ~90 s (Singer et al. 2024 Bayesian meta-analysis); in trained men, 3-min rest beat 1-min for both 1RM strength and some muscle-thickness sites (Schoenfeld 2016). The effect appears to run through preserved per-set volume/load, so short rest is acceptable for isolation work if reps are maintained, and 2-3+ min is advisable for heavy compounds.

### Cited Findings
- Singer, Wolf, Schoenfeld et al. 2024 ("Give it a rest," Frontiers in Sports and Active Living, 14 Aug 2024; Bayesian meta-analysis of randomized rest-interval studies with other variables controlled): "small hypertrophic benefit to employing inter-set rest interval durations >60 s, perhaps mediated by reductions in volume load"; "did not detect appreciable differences in hypertrophy when resting >90 s between sets"; whole-body estimate closer to zero and marginally favouring shorter rest (CrI spanning zero); failure vs non-failure did not change the rest-hypertrophy interaction. No strength outcomes were reported in the retrieved abstract. The paper notes the NSCA still recommends 30-90 s for hypertrophy. — [Frontiers full text](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/sports-and-active-living/articles/10.3389/fspor.2024.1429789/text); [DOAJ record](https://doaj.org/article/18363f4031e0487793db2084bee23f38); [Biolayne REPS 28](https://biolayne.com/reps/issue-28/which-between-set-rest-interval-is-best-for-muscle-growth/)
- Grgic, Lazinica, Mikulic, Krieger & Schoenfeld 2017 (Eur J Sport Sci 17(8); systematic review): both short and long rest viable for untrained; "a possible advantage for the use of long rest intervals" in trained participants with sensitive hypertrophy measures. — [Victoria Univ. repository](https://vuir.vu.edu.au/44281/); [ResearchGate PDF](https://researchgate.net/profile/Brad_Schoenfeld/publication/317832672_The_effects_of_short_versus_long_inter-set_rest_intervals_in_resistance_training_on_measures_of_muscle_hypertrophy_A_systematic_review/links/59a010ad0f7e9b18359db18f/The-effects-of-short-versus-long-inter-set-rest-intervals-in-resistance-training-on-measures-of-muscle-hypertrophy-A-systematic-review.pdf)
- Schoenfeld et al. 2016 (JSCR 30:1805-1812; 21 trained men, 8 wk, 1-min vs 3-min rest, other variables equal): 3-min group gained more 1RM squat and bench; muscle thickness differences significant for anterior thigh with trends favouring longer rest elsewhere (~1.2-3.5 mm reported by one summary); muscular endurance similar. The viral "93% more growth" figure is not documented. — [NASE summary](https://www.naseinc.com/blog/how-long-should-you-rest-between-sets-for-size-and-strength/); [Galaxus summary with citation](https://www.galaxus.de/en/page/how-long-a-break-is-needed-between-sets-during-strength-training-23383)

### Inferences
- Default: 2-3 min on compounds (strength and volume preservation), 60-90 s on isolation/machine work. Going below 60 s costs reps per set and therefore effective volume.
- For the lifter's time-constrained 3-day weeks, antagonist supersets with ~60-90 s between movements preserve the >60 s per-muscle rest while cutting session time (inference; not directly tested in the meta-analysis).

### Gaps
- Singer 2024 numeric effect sizes (reported by one blog as SMD 0.48 short vs 0.56 long) could not be verified against the paper.
- No meta-analysis quantifies rest-interval effects on strength specifically; the strength conclusion rests on Schoenfeld 2016 and mechanistic reasoning.

## Key Question 6: Minimum effective dose to maintain strength and muscle (low-frequency weeks)

### Takeaway
In younger adults, muscle size was maintained for up to 32 weeks on 1 session/week with 1 set/exercise, and strength was retained even through complete detraining over 32 weeks (Bickel 2011; Spiering 2021 review); older adults need ~2 sessions and 2-3 sets. Intensity (load) is the variable that must be preserved; volume can drop by two-thirds to nine-tenths. For a 3-day week, maintaining ~1/3 of normal volume at normal loads is more than enough to hold adaptations.

### Cited Findings
- Spiering, Mujika, Sharp & Foulis 2021 (JSCR 35(5):1449-1458; narrative review): muscle size in younger people maintained for up to 32 weeks with as little as 1 session/week and 1 set/exercise; older people likely need up to 2 sessions/week and 2-3 sets/exercise; endurance maintained up to 15 weeks at 2 sessions/week or 33-66% volume reduction if intensity is kept; "intensity appears to be the key variable for maintaining performance"; insufficient data for athletes/military. — [PubMed 33629972](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33629972/)
- Bickel, Cross & Bamman 2011 (MSSE; flagged older foundational; 70 adults): 16 weeks of 3x/week RT, then 32 weeks randomized to detraining, 1/3 dose, or 1/9 dose. Young (20-35): both 1/3 and 1/9 doses preserved myofiber hypertrophy; the 1/3 dose produced additional hypertrophy. Older (60-75): maintenance doses did not preserve hypertrophy; "older adults require a higher dose of weekly loading than the young to maintain myofiber hypertrophy." Strength from phase 1 was largely retained through detraining with only slight reduction at 32 weeks. The 1/3 dose better maintained the IIx-to-IIa fibre shift. — [PubMed 21131862](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21131862/); [full PDF](https://paulogentil.com/pdf/Exercise%20Dosing%20to%20Retain%20Resistance%20Training%20Adaptations%20in%20Young%20and%20Older%20Adults.pdf)
- Androulakis-Korakakis 2020/2021 minimum-effective-dose work (trained men / powerlifters): 1 set of 6-12 reps at 70-85% 1RM, 2-3x/week, or ~3-6 heavy sets of 1-5 reps per week at >80% 1RM, still increased 1RM over 6-12 weeks. — [Substack summary of 2020](https://shortcutu.substack.com/p/setting-the-bar-low-but-still-making); [Frontiers 2021](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/sports-and-active-living/articles/10.3389/fspor.2021.713655/epub)
- RP maintenance volume (MV) for chest: ~4-6 sets/week for experienced trainers, consistent with the maintenance literature. — [RP help: Chest](https://help.rpstrength.com/hc/en-us/articles/32433153518359-Chest)

### Inferences
- A 3-day week that keeps each muscle at ~4-6 hard sets at normal loads (1/3 of a 12-18-set week) will maintain and likely still slowly build size and strength in an intermediate under 40; it is not a "lost week."
- Because intensity is the maintenance lever, the 3-day week should keep the top sets heavy (RPE 8-9 on main lifts) and cut accessory sets rather than lightening loads.
- The maintenance data come from untrained-to-trained 16-week subjects; truly advanced lifters probably need more than 1/9 but the 1/3 result is a safe floor.

### Gaps
- No maintenance study in intermediate/advanced lifters beyond 16 weeks of prior training; Spiering notes no data for athletes.
- Exact strength-retention percentages at 1/3 and 1/9 dose in Bickel were not in retrieved text.

## Key Question 7: Redistributing weekly volume from 3 to 6 sessions: spreading vs concentrating, recovery per session

### Takeaway
With weekly sets equal, spreading them over 6 sessions vs 3 produces the same hypertrophy and strength in trained lifters (Colquhoun 2018; Saric 2019; Hamarsland 2022 for 2 vs 4), so the 6-day week's advantage is capacity: it allows more weekly sets while keeping each session under the ~8-11 sets/muscle per-session ceiling (Remmert 2025), with higher per-set quality and slightly better strength (Pelland). The 3-day week should therefore run lower total volume (~1/2 to 2/3) rather than cramming 6 days of sets into 3.

### Cited Findings
- Colquhoun 2018 (trained men): same weekly work split 3x vs 6x (two sessions/day in the 6x arm) produced similar strength and body-composition gains over 6 weeks. — [ResearchGate PDF](https://researchgate.net/profile/Jozo-Grgic/publication/327435932_Resistance_Training_Frequencies_of_3_and_6_Times_Per_Week_Produce_Similar_Muscular_Adaptations_in_Resistance-Trained_Men/links/5bd26d6892851c6b278f311d/Resistance-Training-Frequencies-of-3-and-6-Times-Per-Week-Produce-Similar-Muscular-Adaptations-in-Resistance-Trained-Men.pdf); [IDEA Fit](https://www.ideafit.com/volume-is-key-factor-in-building-strength)
- Hamarsland 2022 introduction (review of volume-equated trained studies): no difference 3 vs 6 (Colquhoun; Saric 2019), 2 vs 4 (Johnsen & van den Tillaar 2021), 1 vs 5 (Gomes 2019); exception Zaroni 2019 favouring higher frequency. — [Frontiers](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/physiology/articles/10.3389/fphys.2021.789403/full)
- Remmert 2025 (per-session PUOS ~11 fractional sets hypertrophy, ~2 direct sets strength) implies that above ~11 sets/muscle/session additional sets are undetectable, i.e., a 3-day week caps productive volume at roughly 2 sessions x ~10 sets = ~20 sets/muscle/week only if every muscle is hit twice. — [SportRxiv 537](https://sportrxiv.org/index.php/server/preprint/view/537); [Biolayne REPS 36](https://biolayne.com/reps/issue-36/too-much-muscle-math-finding-the-ideal-training-volume-per-session/)
- Pelland: frequency independently (modestly) helps strength with diminishing returns; Stronger By Science: higher frequency's main benefit is enabling higher volume and higher average training quality. — [train.fitness](https://train.fitness/personal-trainer-blogs/not-all-sets-are-created-equal); [SBS](https://www.strongerbyscience.com/frequency/)
- RP chest MRV rises from ~20 sets/week at 2 sessions to ~25 at 3 sessions, i.e., recoverable weekly volume scales with the number of sessions the volume is split across. — [RP chest guide](https://rpstrength.com/chest-training-tips-hypertrophy/)
- Räntilä 2026: in trained athletes, 1.5x volume (~60 sets/wk) did not improve size and hurt deadlift, a caution against using 6 days simply to add sets. — [JYX](https://jyx.jyu.fi/handle/123456789/112971)
- Enes 2024: progressively adding 4-6 sets/week (from 22 up to ~52 quad sets/week) improved strength more than constant volume in trained males over 12 weeks (hypertrophy uncertain). — [BarBend](https://barbend.com/news/you-dont-need-more-training-volume/)

### Inferences
- Template for the alternating lifter: 6-day week (e.g., PPL x2 or upper/lower x3): each muscle 2-3x/week, 5-8 hard sets per muscle per session, 14-20 sets/muscle/week, main lifts 2-3x/week at 2-5 heavy sets. 3-day week (full-body x3 or upper/lower/full): each muscle 2-3x/week, 3-5 sets per muscle per session, 8-12 sets/muscle/week, main lifts 2x/week. Keep loads/RPE identical across both week types; cut accessory sets, not intensity.
- Do not map 6-day volume onto 3 days: 18 sets of quads in one session exceeds Remmert's PUOS by ~60%, and the maintenance literature shows 1/3 volume is sufficient to hold gains, so the 3-day week can be deliberately sub-maximal.
- Expected cost of the 3-day weeks: near-zero for hypertrophy if they are a minority of weeks and volume stays ≥8-10 sets/muscle; a small strength-practice cost that is offset by hitting main lifts 2x in the 3 days.

### Gaps
- No study has tested alternating high- and low-frequency weeks within the same program; the recommendation is assembled from volume-equated frequency trials plus maintenance data.
- No trial directly measures within-session fatigue decay set-by-set in a way that pins the per-session ceiling to a specific number for compounds vs isolations.
